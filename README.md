# Global AI Talent Flow

*Do countries that train AI researchers retain them, or does talent concentrate in a few destination countries?*

Persona/Target Audience: **A government policy analyst** who wants to understand where AI researchers are trained, where they work, and which countries retain or attract researchers. The app helps the analyst compare these patterns and identify areas that may need further investigation when developing education and talent policies.


## User Stories

Story #1:

As a government policy analyst, I want to compare where AI researchers completed their undergraduate education with where they currently work, so that I can understand which countries train more researchers than they employ and which countries attract researchers trained elsewhere.
Looking at the 'Train vs Work' visualisation, I can see that the USA has the largest positive difference, with 2,145 more researchers working there than those who completed their undergraduate education there. On the other hand, China has the largest negative difference, with 1,729 fewer researchers working there compared to the number it trained. The visualisation allows me to switch between percentages and actual researcher counts, compare training and employment side by side, and focus on smaller countries such as Australia and Singapore.
This helps me understand how AI research talent is distributed across different countries. It also shows which countries may need to focus more on retaining their researchers and which countries could benefit from attracting talent from overseas.

Story #2:

The perspective it serves is to show a government policy analyst whether countries that train the most AI researchers also kept the largest share, and along with it how many graduates from each country sends abroad, so that I can tell whether a low retention rate is a big loss of people or only a small-base effect before deciding where to focus retention policy.

By looking at the ‘Size vs retention’ visualisation, it can be seen that China trained the most researchers (5,903) and managed retained 68.7% of them, meanwhile Australia retained 47.5% from 59 researchers. When bubble area tries to show graduates now working elsewhere, China has by far the largest bubble (1,847 working abroad) even though its rate is above Australia’s, while India’s possess a much lower rate (10.0%) rests on 370 graduates working somewhere else. Within the sample, training more researchers does not have any impact on keeping more of researchers. This is reflected by USA who retained 88.7% of 1,369, while China only retained 68.7% of 5,903. In conclusion, a low rate on a small base, such as Australia’s, and a moderate rate on a large base, such as China’s, are two different retention problems that need to be followed-up each on its own respective.

Story #3:

As a government policy analyst, I want to see where researchers trained in a country end up working, and where a country’s workforce trained, so that I can tell whether researchers who move are spread across many destinations or concentrated in one, and which country a retention policy would be competing against.

Looking at the ‘Where do researchers from (or in) a country end up?’ visualisation, I can see that of the 5,903 researchers who did their undergraduate degree in China, 1,847 now work elsewhere. The published career paths name the destination for 1,406 of them: 1,179 (63.8% of China’s leavers) work in the USA, 147 in Singapore and 80 in Europe. The other 441 are shown as “destination not published” and are not guessed. The USA is also the main destination overall. 2,300 of its 3,514 researchers trained elsewhere, which is 63.4% of all 3,626 cross-geography moves in the cohort. India shows the same pattern: all 246 of its named leavers (out of 370) work in the USA.

Within this sample, researchers who move are concentrated in one destination, so retention policy in countries like China and India would be competing mainly with the USA. The destinations come from 18 selected career paths covering 79.9% of the cohort, and they show affiliation, not verified migration.

Story #4:

As a technology policy minister in Australia, I want to see the ratio of homegrown versus imported AI researchers in our current workforce, so that I can determine whether we need to expand specialised visa programs to remain globally competitive or increase funding for domestic STEM education.

Looking at the 'Workforce Composition' visualisation, I can see that countries like China and South Korea mainly rely on their homegrown talent to supply their AI workforce. On the other hand, the visualisation also shows that countries like the USA and Singapore rely heavily on researchers from overseas for their AI Workforce. The situation in Australia is similar to the latter, where there are more AI researchers from overseas compared to homegrown AI researchers. However, this is from a small baseline. Moving forward, it makes sense to increase funding for domestic programs to increase our homegrown AI workforce and remain globally competitive in the near future.
